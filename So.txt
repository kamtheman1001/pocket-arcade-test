Game Development Concept: Clash Royale-Style Real-Time Strategy Game

1. General Game Concept

I want to create a real-time multiplayer strategy game inspired by Clash Royale. The game combines elements of tower defense, collectible cards, resource management, and competitive PvP combat.

The player controls an army indirectly by placing cards onto a battlefield. Each card represents a troop, building, or spell with different abilities, statistics, and costs.

Instead of directly controlling characters, players make strategic decisions about:

* Which cards to deploy.
* Where to deploy them.
* When to spend resources.
* Which enemy units to counter.
* Whether to attack or defend.
* How to combine units to create powerful attacks.

Battles should be relatively short, lasting approximately 3–5 minutes.

The game must feel fast-paced, responsive, strategic, and visually satisfying. Every troop should move, attack, take damage, and react independently once deployed.

The most important design principle is that the player controls the strategy, while the game automatically handles individual unit behavior.

⸻

2. Battlefield and Arena Design

The main battlefield should be a rectangular arena viewed from an elevated, slightly angled, top-down perspective.

The arena is divided horizontally into two territories.

The bottom half belongs to the local player, and the top half belongs to their opponent.

Arena Layout

The battlefield contains:

* Two opposing player territories.
* A river separating both territories.
* Two bridges connecting the territories.
* Two main combat lanes.
* Six total towers, three belonging to each player.
* Designated placement zones for deploying units.

Each player owns three towers:

Left Defense Tower

Located toward the front-left side of the player’s territory.

Right Defense Tower

Located toward the front-right side of the player’s territory.

King Tower

Located toward the back-center of the player’s territory.

The two smaller towers automatically attack approaching enemies.

The King Tower is a stronger central defensive structure that initially remains inactive. It begins attacking after receiving damage or after one of its defending towers has been destroyed.

Battlefield Behavior

Units should normally navigate toward the enemy using one of the two bridges.

Ground troops should not freely cross the river except by using a valid bridge or a special movement ability.

Flying troops can ignore the river and move directly toward their targets.

The environment should contain clear visual boundaries showing where cards may be deployed.

Normally, players may deploy troops only within their own territory.

After destroying an opponent’s side tower, additional placement territory becomes available on that side of the battlefield.

This allows the player to deploy units deeper into enemy territory, creating an advantage for successful attacks.

The arena should be symmetrical for both players to maintain fair gameplay.

⸻

3. Main Objective and Winning Conditions

The primary objective is to destroy more enemy towers than the opponent while protecting your own.

Each destroyed side tower counts as one crown.

Destroying the enemy King Tower immediately ends the battle and gives the attacking player a three-crown victory.

Standard Match Rules

A standard match consists of:

Regular Battle: 3 minutes

Both players deploy cards, defend their towers, and attempt to destroy enemy towers.

Overtime: Up to 2 minutes

If both players have the same number of crowns after regular time expires, the game enters sudden-death overtime.

The first player to destroy another enemy tower wins.

Tiebreaker

If no tower is destroyed during overtime, the game compares remaining tower health to determine a winner using a health-based tiebreaker.

Exact ties can be handled according to the chosen game mode.

Match End Conditions

The match ends when:

1. A player’s King Tower is destroyed.
2. Regular time expires with one player holding more crowns.
3. A player destroys a tower during sudden-death overtime.
4. The overtime clock expires and a tiebreaker determines the winner.

After the match ends, all troops should stop moving, the camera should emphasize the result, and a victory or defeat screen should appear.

⸻

4. Elixir and Resource Management

Elixir is the primary resource used during battles.

Every card has an elixir cost, which determines how expensive it is to deploy.

Elixir regenerates automatically over time.

The player’s current elixir amount is displayed as a horizontal resource bar at the bottom of the screen.

Elixir Mechanics

* Maximum elixir capacity: 10.
* Players start with a partially filled elixir bar.
* Elixir regenerates continuously.
* Playing a card immediately subtracts its cost.
* A player cannot deploy a card without sufficient elixir.
* Elixir regeneration pauses at the maximum capacity until some is spent.

For example:

A player has 8 elixir.

They deploy a troop costing 5 elixir.

Their remaining elixir becomes 3.

The bar continues regenerating, allowing them to deploy additional cards.

Elixir Regeneration Phases

For standard battles, implement the following regeneration phases:

Match Phase	Regeneration
First minute	Normal (1x)
Second minute	Normal (1x)
Third minute	Double (2x)
First overtime minute	Double (2x)
Final overtime minute	Triple (3x)

The progression of elixir generation is extremely important.

Early gameplay should encourage careful resource spending and smaller engagements.

Late gameplay should allow players to deploy more troops and create significantly larger battles.

Resource Strategy

A cheap troop should be capable of countering a more expensive troop under the right circumstances.

For example, a 3-elixir defensive unit might defeat a 5-elixir attacking unit if placed correctly.

This creates a positive elixir trade and gives the defending player a resource advantage.

The game should reward effective resource management rather than simply deploying the strongest available units.

5. Card and Deck System

The player should build a battle deck containing eight cards.

Each card represents an action that can be performed in battle.

There are three primary card categories:

Troop Cards

Summon one or more units that automatically move and attack.

Examples include:

* Melee warriors.
* Ranged archers.
* Heavy armored tanks.
* Flying creatures.
* Fast attacking units.
* Swarms of weaker units.
* Support units with special abilities.
* Units that specifically target buildings.

Building Cards

Place a defensive or utility structure onto the battlefield.

Examples include:

* Defensive cannons.
* Archer towers.
* Mortar-like structures.
* Troop-spawning buildings.
* Resource-generating structures.

Buildings should generally have a limited lifetime and may also be destroyed by enemy attacks.

Spell Cards

Activate an immediate or temporary effect in a selected area.

Examples include:

* Direct damage explosions.
* Lightning strikes.
* Area-of-effect damage.
* Slowing fields.
* Freezing effects.
* Healing or regeneration effects.
* Knockback effects.
* Temporary stat boosts.

Unlike troops, spells usually do not create a permanent moving unit.

⸻

6. Card Hand and Cycle Mechanics

Although the player owns eight cards in their deck, only four should be available to play at a time.

The four active cards appear horizontally at the bottom of the screen.

A smaller indicator displays which card will appear next.

Card Cycle Behavior

At the beginning of the match:

1. The eight-card deck is shuffled into an initial order.
2. Four cards are displayed in the active hand.
3. The remaining cards form a queue.
4. When the player deploys a card, it leaves their hand.
5. The next card in the queue replaces it.
6. The played card is placed at the back of the cycle.
7. After cycling through four other cards, the previously used card becomes available again.

The player should never have access to all eight cards simultaneously.

This system is essential because it introduces strategic limitations.

For example, the player might have a strong counter to an enemy unit but cannot use it until they cycle through enough cards.

The interface should display the current card hand, elixir costs, and next card clearly.

Cards should visually indicate whether the player has enough elixir to use them.

⸻

7. Card Deployment System

Players should be able to deploy cards through a simple drag-and-drop interaction.

Deployment Process

1. The player selects a card from their hand.
2. The card becomes highlighted.
3. The player drags toward the arena.
4. A translucent placement preview appears.
5. The game checks whether the position is valid.
6. If valid, releasing the card deploys the unit or activates the effect.
7. Elixir is deducted.
8. The card leaves the active hand.
9. The next card enters the hand.

Placement Restrictions

Troops and buildings should have restricted deployment areas.

Spell cards may have different targeting rules and often can be used across the whole arena.

Invalid locations should appear highlighted in red.

Valid locations should display a normal placement indicator.

If the player releases a card in an invalid location, it should return to their hand without spending elixir.

Troop Spawn Delay

Troops should have a brief deployment animation before entering combat.

This prevents units from appearing and instantly attacking with no visual feedback.

Certain cards can have special deployment effects, such as jumping into the arena, spawning underground, or dropping from the sky.

⸻

8. Troop AI and Automatic Movement

Every deployed troop should operate independently using an automated AI system.

Players do not manually control movement, attacks, or targeting after deployment.

Required Unit Statistics

Each troop should have configurable attributes including:

Attribute	Purpose
Health	Damage the unit can receive
Damage	Damage inflicted per attack
Attack speed	Time between attacks
Movement speed	Movement rate
Attack range	Distance from which it can attack
Detection range	Distance used to find targets
Target preference	Which enemies it prioritizes
Attack type	Melee, ranged, or special
Movement type	Ground or flying
Elixir cost	Deployment cost
Deployment count	Number of units spawned
Special ability	Optional unique behavior

Basic AI Behavior

Each troop should follow this general process:

State 1: Spawning

The troop appears at the designated location and plays its spawning animation.

State 2: Searching

The troop evaluates nearby objects and searches for a valid target.

State 3: Moving

If no valid target is within attack range, the troop moves toward its selected target.

State 4: Attacking

When the troop reaches attack range, it stops moving if necessary and begins attacking.

State 5: Retargeting

If the current target dies, disappears, or becomes invalid, the troop selects another valid target.

State 6: Death

When health reaches zero, the troop plays a death animation, stops participating in combat, and is removed.

Troop Targeting

Troops should not randomly attack everything.

Each troop has a targeting preference.

Examples:

Standard Melee Unit

Attacks the closest valid enemy troop or building.

Building-Focused Unit

Ignores ordinary enemy troops and attempts to reach enemy buildings or towers.

Anti-Air Unit

Can attack flying units and ground units.

Ground-Only Unit

Cannot target flying enemies.

Ranged Unit

Attempts to attack enemies from a distance rather than moving directly into melee range.

Splash-Damage Unit

Damages multiple enemies within an area.

Pathfinding

Ground troops should navigate around obstacles and toward valid river crossings.

Units may need to move around other troops, buildings, and environmental barriers.

The system should prevent units from becoming permanently stuck.

Flying troops should use a different navigation system that allows them to cross the river directly.

Pathfinding should be efficient enough to handle dozens of moving units simultaneously.

9. Combat and Damage System

Combat should happen automatically once troops are within range of valid targets.

Each attack should use the unit’s damage, attack speed, and any special modifiers.

Basic Attack Sequence

1. Identify a valid enemy.
2. Move into attack range.
3. Begin the attack animation.
4. Apply damage at the correct animation moment.
5. Trigger hit effects.
6. Wait for the attack cooldown.
7. Repeat until the target is destroyed or becomes invalid.

Attack Types

Melee Attacks

Require the attacker to be close to the target.

Projectile Attacks

Create a projectile that travels toward the selected target.

Area-of-Effect Attacks

Apply damage to multiple enemies within a defined radius.

Piercing Attacks

Allow a projectile to damage multiple enemies along its path.

Chain Attacks

Bounce between nearby enemies.

Damage-Over-Time Attacks

Apply damage repeatedly over a duration.

Status Effects

Some troops and spells should apply temporary effects such as:

* Slow.
* Stun.
* Freeze.
* Poison.
* Burn.
* Knockback.
* Rage or attack-speed boosts.
* Healing.
* Shields.

These effects should be controlled through a centralized status-effect system.

For example, a frozen troop should stop moving and attacking until the effect expires.

Different effects should have configurable durations, strengths, stacking rules, and immunities.

Collision and Hit Detection

The game should use consistent collision boundaries for all troops and towers.

Projectile hits, melee ranges, area damage, and spell impacts should be evaluated by the authoritative game simulation.

Visual effects must match actual gameplay calculations.

⸻

10. Tower Mechanics

Towers are the most important defensive structures.

Each player has two side towers and one King Tower.

Side Towers

Side towers should:

* Have a health bar.
* Automatically detect enemies.
* Attack valid enemies within range.
* Use a configurable attack speed.
* Display damage effects.
* Collapse when health reaches zero.

King Tower

The King Tower should:

* Have significantly more health.
* Be positioned behind the side towers.
* Initially remain inactive.
* Activate when directly damaged or when a friendly side tower is destroyed.
* Attack enemies once activated.
* Immediately trigger defeat if destroyed.

Tower Targeting

Towers should prioritize valid enemies within their attack range.

Targeting should be predictable so players can deliberately use cheap units to distract towers while stronger units attack.

The game may also support different tower weapon configurations.

For example:

* Fast-attacking tower with moderate damage.
* Slow-attacking tower with high damage.
* Area-damage tower.
* Support-oriented tower with a unique passive ability.

Tower health and attack statistics should scale according to the player’s progression or according to standardized competitive levels.

⸻

11. Unit Roles and Deck Strategy

Cards should have specific strategic roles.

The goal is to make deck building an important part of the game.

Tanks

High-health units that absorb damage.

Usually move slowly and cost more elixir.

Damage Dealers

Units that deal high damage but may have limited health.

They work best behind tanks or when protected.

Swarm Units

Deploy multiple weaker troops.

Effective against single-target attackers but vulnerable to area damage.

Support Units

Provide ranged attacks, healing, crowd control, or special effects.

Win Conditions

Cards designed to reach and damage enemy towers.

These are the primary offensive tools around which a deck is built.

Defensive Cards

Buildings and specialized troops that counter enemy attacks.

Cycle Cards

Low-cost cards that let the player rotate through the deck quickly.

A well-designed deck should combine offense, defense, and utility.

No single card should consistently defeat every other card.

13. Advanced Card Abilities

After the basic game works, I want to introduce cards with more complicated mechanics.

Examples include:

Charge Attacks

A troop accelerates toward an enemy and deals bonus damage on its first hit.

Death Effects

A troop drops an explosive, creates smaller troops, or activates another effect when killed.

Summoning

A unit periodically creates additional friendly troops.

Transformation

A troop changes into a stronger or different form when a particular condition is met.

Jumping

A troop can leap over obstacles or directly toward a target.

Burrowing

A troop can move underground and appear near a designated position.

Shields

A troop has an additional protective health layer that must be destroyed before its normal health can be damaged.

These behaviors should be modular.

The same ability system should support multiple cards rather than requiring completely separate code for every troop.

14. Evolutions and Hero Cards

I also want special versions of certain cards that introduce additional strategic possibilities.

Evolved Cards

Some cards should have unlockable evolved forms.

An evolved form could gain:

* A new attack.
* An additional status effect.
* Increased durability.
* A unique passive ability.
* Different deployment behavior.

Evolutions can activate after the player cycles the card a specified number of times.

For example, after one normal deployment and the required cycle, the next deployment may use the evolved version.

The active card should visually change so the player knows its evolved form is ready.

Hero Cards

Hero cards should have abilities that the player can activate manually during a battle.

After deploying a hero, an ability button appears near the player’s battle controls.

Pressing it activates the hero’s ability, provided any required conditions or resources are available.

Hero abilities might include:

* Temporary invulnerability.
* A powerful area attack.
* Teleportation.
* Summoning reinforcements.
* Taunting nearby enemies.
* Temporarily increasing attack speed.

Special cards should be assigned through dedicated deck slots so their use can be limited and balanced.

These systems should come after the core troop, spell, and building mechanics are stable.

The user interface should be designed primarily for a vertical mobile screen, with the arena occupying most of the display.

Battle HUD

Top of Screen

* Opponent username.
* Opponent tower health values.
* Remaining match time.
* Crown score.
* Overtime or special battle indicators.

Center of Screen

* Interactive battlefield.
* Troops and buildings.
* Projectiles.
* Towers.
* Unit health bars.
* Deployment previews.
* Combat effects.

Bottom of Screen

* Four playable cards.
* Current elixir.
* Elixir regeneration bar.
* Next-card preview.
* Active hero ability button, when applicable.

Visual Feedback

Cards should become dimmed when the player cannot afford them.

Dragging a card should create a placement preview.

The elixir bar should animate smoothly.

Towers should visibly react when taking damage.

Important events such as tower destruction, double elixir, and overtime should display clear screen notifications.

The UI should be readable without obstructing the battlefield.

Home Screen

17. Multiplayer Networking

Real-time multiplayer is a fundamental requirement.

Both players must see the same battle state, with consistent unit positions, health values, resource spending, and combat results.

Server-Authoritative Architecture

I want the multiplayer system to be server-authoritative.

This means the server determines important gameplay outcomes rather than blindly trusting the player’s device.

The server should control:

* Match timers.
* Elixir regeneration.
* Card deployment validation.
* Troop spawning.
* Unit movement.
* Target acquisition.
* Damage calculations.
* Tower destruction.
* Match results.

The clients should primarily handle player input, rendering, sound, and visual effects.

Example Deployment Flow

1. Player A drags a card onto the battlefield.
2. Their client sends a deployment request to the server.
3. The server verifies the selected card is available.
4. The server checks the player’s elixir.
5. The server validates the placement location.
6. If valid, the server deducts elixir and spawns the unit.
7. The server updates the card cycle.
8. Both players receive the new battle state.
9. Both devices display the troop appearing.

Networking Requirements

The game should handle:

* Latency.
* Packet loss.
* Player disconnections.
* Temporary reconnects.
* Duplicate input requests.
* Synchronization errors.
* Abnormally delayed client actions.

A fixed simulation tick rate, for example 20–30 updates per second, could be used initially.

Clients should interpolate movement between updates to keep animations smooth.

Deployment responsiveness is especially important because even a small delay can change the outcome of a defensive interaction.

18. Matchmaking and Game Modes

The game should initially support competitive 1v1 battles.

1v1 Battles

Two players are matched based on comparable skill or progression.

Both enter the same arena and use their selected decks.

Friendly Battles

Players can invite friends to private matches without affecting their competitive ranking.

Training Matches

Players battle an AI-controlled opponent.

This mode should help new players learn card mechanics.

Ranked Battles

Players compete to gain trophies or ranking points.

Winning increases their rating, while losing may decrease it.

The matchmaking system should attempt to find opponents of similar skill.

Future Modes

Possible additional modes include:

* 2v2 battles.
* Double-elixir battles.
* Triple-elixir battles.
* Draft mode.
* Randomized decks.
* Limited-card challenges.
* Tournament mode.
* Special-event arenas.

The basic 1v1 system should be completed before additional modes are introduced.

19. AI Opponents

I want AI opponents for testing, tutorials, and optional practice.

The AI should use the same card and elixir mechanics as real players.

It should not have unlimited resources or bypass standard placement rules.

AI Decision-Making

The AI should periodically evaluate:

* Current elixir.
* Available cards.
* Enemy troops.
* Tower health.
* Enemy attacks.
* Available deployment positions.
* Offensive opportunities.

The AI should use this information to choose an action.

For example, if an enemy tank is approaching the left tower, the AI could deploy an appropriate defensive troop or building.

If the opponent has spent most of their elixir, the AI could choose to attack the opposite lane.

Difficulty Levels

Easy

Makes basic decisions and reacts slowly.

Medium

Uses reasonable counters and manages elixir.

Hard

Tracks enemy cards, makes efficient trades, and builds coordinated attacks.

Expert

Uses more advanced deck cycling, positioning, prediction, and offensive timing.

20. Progression and Unlocking

Outside of battles, the player should work toward unlocking new cards, upgrading their collection, and progressing through different arenas.

Trophy System

Winning competitive matches grants trophies.

Losing may remove trophies.

As players reach trophy milestones, they unlock new arenas, rewards, and cards.

Arena Progression

Each arena should have a distinct visual theme.

Examples:

* Training Grounds.
* Forest Arena.
* Desert Arena.
* Frozen Fortress.
* Volcanic Battlefield.
* Royal Citadel.

Arenas should generally use the same underlying gameplay geometry unless a specific game mode intentionally changes it.

This helps maintain competitive balance.

Card Upgrading

Cards should have levels.

Higher levels can increase statistics such as health and damage.

Players use collected card copies and in-game currency to upgrade cards.

The maximum level should be configurable so the progression system can evolve.

Card Rarity

Cards can be divided into categories such as:

* Common.
* Rare.
* Epic.
* Legendary.
* Champion.

Rarity should influence acquisition and design complexity without automatically making a card unbeatable.

The game should have a colorful, polished, stylized appearance.

Individual units need distinctive silhouettes so players can recognize them quickly.

Required Animations

* Idle.
* Spawn.
* Walk or fly.
* Attack.
* Taking damage.
* Death.
* Special ability.

Towers should support:

* Idle.
* Attacking.
* Taking damage.
* Destruction.

Combat Effects

Combat should include:

* Projectile trails.
* Impact flashes.
* Explosion particles.
* Small knockback reactions.
* Damage indicators where appropriate.
* Status-effect visuals.
* Destruction debris.
* Screen shake for important events.

Animation Principles

Animations must reflect gameplay timing.

A projectile should not cause visible damage significantly before reaching its target.

The game should prioritize clarity and responsiveness over unnecessary animation complexity.

24. Technical System Architecture

I would prefer the game to use modular, reusable systems rather than one large script controlling everything.

The major systems should be separated into independent components.

Core Managers

MatchManager

Controls match initialization, timers, match phases, and win conditions.

ElixirManager

Handles resource generation, maximum capacity, and spending.

DeckManager

Stores deck composition, active hand, card cycle, and next-card queue.

CardManager

Loads card definitions and handles card-related actions.

DeploymentManager

Validates and processes placement requests.

UnitManager

Creates, tracks, and removes active troops.

MovementSystem

Handles troop navigation, movement speeds, and positioning.

TargetingSystem

Determines which entities a troop can attack.

CombatSystem

Processes attacks, damage, projectiles, and health changes.

EffectSystem

Handles buffs, debuffs, special abilities, and temporary effects.

TowerManager

Controls defensive towers, tower targeting, and destruction.

NetworkManager

Handles multiplayer communication and synchronization.

ProgressionManager

Tracks player levels, trophies, unlocks, and upgrades.

UIManager

Updates interface elements and handles menus.

Data-Driven Card Definitions

Every card should have a reusable data structure.

For example:

{
  "id": "iron_guardian",
  "name": "Iron Guardian",
  "type": "troop",
  "rarity": "rare",
  "elixirCost": 5,
  "health": 1800,
  "damage": 160,
  "attackInterval": 1.5,
  "movementSpeed": 1.2,
  "attackRange": 1.0,
  "targetType": "ground",
  "targetPriority": "nearest",
  "movementType": "ground",
  "spawnCount": 1,
  "ability": null
}

These are example development values, not final balancing numbers.

The goal is to be able to add a new troop primarily by defining its stats, visual assets, and special behaviors.

The core engine should not require substantial rewrites whenever a new card is introduced.

25. Game Simulation and Performance

The game should use a consistent simulation model.

Simulation Requirements

* All gameplay calculations use consistent time steps.
* Attacks and cooldowns are not dependent on rendering frame rate.
* Units cannot attack after dying.
* Dead targets are removed from targeting systems.
* Troops should not attack out-of-range enemies.
* Elixir cannot become negative.
* Invalid deployments should be rejected.
* Multiple attacks occurring on the same simulation tick should resolve consistently.

Performance

The game should remain smooth when many troops are active simultaneously.

Possible optimizations include:

* Object pooling for projectiles and effects.
* Efficient spatial queries.
* Limited-frequency target searches.
* Simplified collision calculations.
* Efficient pathfinding updates.
* Animation culling for off-screen objects.
* Separating visual effects from gameplay calculations.

The game should remain playable on lower-powered devices.

26. Testing and Balancing

Gameplay balance is essential.

Every card should have strengths, weaknesses, and reasonable counters.

Important Testing Scenarios

Deployment Tests

Confirm that cards cannot be played without enough elixir or outside valid placement areas.

Card Cycle Tests

Confirm that played cards return to the hand in the correct order.

Targeting Tests

Confirm that ground-only troops ignore flying units and building-focused troops prioritize buildings.

Pathfinding Tests

Confirm that troops cross bridges correctly and do not become trapped.

Combat Tests

Confirm that damage, attack speed, and status effects work correctly.

Tower Tests

Confirm tower activation, automatic targeting, and destruction behavior.

Match Tests

Confirm victory, overtime, tiebreakers, and simultaneous destruction events.

Multiplayer Tests

Confirm both players receive consistent match states.

Disconnect Tests

Confirm the game behaves predictably when a player loses connection.

Balance Metrics

The game should record useful statistics such as:

* Card usage rates.
* Card win rates.
* Average match duration.
* Average elixir spent.
* Frequency of three-crown wins.
* Average damage dealt by each card.
* Most common deck combinations.

These metrics can help identify cards that are excessively strong or weak.

28. Minimum Viable Product Requirements

Before adding progression systems or advanced content, the game should meet these requirements:

1. Two players or one player and an AI can enter a match.
2. Each player has three functioning towers.
3. Each player has an eight-card deck.
4. Four cards are available in the active hand.
5. Cards cycle correctly when played.
6. Elixir generates and is deducted correctly.
7. Players can deploy cards through drag-and-drop.
8. Troops move and select targets automatically.
9. Ground troops correctly use bridges.
10. Flying troops can cross the river.
11. Units attack and take damage.
12. Spells apply their intended effects.
13. Towers automatically defend themselves.
14. Destroyed towers stop functioning.
15. The King Tower activates appropriately.
16. Match timers and overtime work.
17. Victory and defeat are calculated correctly.
18. The game can be restarted for another match.
19. All major gameplay rules are enforced by the game simulation.
20. The experience remains smooth with multiple active units.

29. Overall Gameplay Experience

The primary objective is to recreate the strategic feel of a fast-paced, lane-based card battler.

The game should not simply be about who has the most powerful cards.

It should reward players who understand timing, resource management, placement, troop interactions, and deck composition.

Every deployment should matter.

A cheap defensive card placed correctly should sometimes be more valuable than an expensive offensive card placed poorly.

Players should constantly decide whether to defend, counterattack, build a larger push, or save elixir for a better opportunity.

The game should create exciting moments when large groups of troops clash, towers barely survive, and a perfectly timed card changes the outcome of a match.

Most importantly, the underlying systems should be flexible enough to support dozens or eventually hundreds of unique cards without requiring the entire game to be rebuilt.

The final goal is a polished, competitive, real-time multiplayer strategy game built around automated troop combat, tactical card placement, limited resources, and tower destruction.

Use original characters, sounds, visuals, and branding while preserving the core strategic principles that make this genre engaging.
